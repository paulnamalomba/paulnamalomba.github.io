Level 4 — Scenario & Behavioural Questions

These are the unpredictable "What would you do if…" questions. Practise thinking on your feet, but anchor every answer in real experience using the STAR framework.

⏱️ Remember: 40-minute interview, 8 panellists

You'll get ~5–6 questions total. Some will be scenario-based. Keep answers to 2 minutes max. If you go longer, the panel loses engagement and you lose questions where you could shine.

🔥 Scenario: Server Crisis During Registration

Immediate response (first 15 minutes): Triage the issue — is it SEAT-specific or a server-level problem? I'd check server status, error logs, and recent deployments. If it's server infrastructure (e.g., Ubuntu server, Apache2), I'd contact ICTS immediately as I have direct relationships with their team. If it's application-level, I'd identify the error, roll back any recent changes if needed, and restart services.

Communication (within 30 minutes): Notify the faculty office (Celeste Wilson) and HOD with a clear status update: what happened, estimated fix time, and workaround if any. Students need to know it's being handled.

Resolution and post-mortem: Fix the root cause, not just the symptom. After restoration, document what happened and implement preventive measures — better monitoring, automated alerts, staging environment for testing changes before deployment.

Real experience: I've maintained 100% SEAT uptime with zero server errors per month. This isn't hypothetical — I've been the person responsible for keeping SEAT running during registration. I know the system, the infrastructure, and who to call.

🔬 Scenario: Conflicting Research Group Demands

Prioritisation framework: I'd assess each request on three factors: urgency (is there a hard deadline?), impact (who's blocked?), and effort (how long will it take?).

In this case:

  • CoMSIRU — HPC access (highest priority): Conference deadline is immovable. This is likely a quick setup task — granting access, helping with job submission scripts. I'd address this first, possibly within a few hours.
  • Future Water — broken pipeline (medium priority): Researchers are blocked. Debugging a Python pipeline is my daily work. I'd aim to diagnose and fix this within a day.
  • Transport — sensor network (lower urgency, higher effort): Setting up a sensor network is a multi-day project with no immediate deadline. I'd schedule a planning meeting this week and begin work next week.

Key point: Transparent communication. I'd let all three groups know the timeline and my reasoning. Nobody likes being deprioritised, but they respect honesty and a clear plan.

👨‍🏫 Scenario: Resistant Academic

Approach: I'd start by listening. A senior professor has valuable context about their course that I may not fully understand. Their resistance might be about workload, scepticism about relevance, or a bad experience with poorly implemented tech in the past.

Strategy:

  • Understand their concerns — Meet one-on-one, not in a committee. Ask what their real objection is.
  • Show, don't tell — Rather than arguing for coding, demonstrate a small, specific example: "Here's how 10 lines of Python could automate the data analysis your students do manually in Excel." Make it about their course outcomes, not about technology for its own sake.
  • Start small — Offer to create a single exercise that integrates seamlessly into their existing structure. If it works, they'll ask for more.
  • Leverage the mandate — The digital design line is a departmental and ECSA requirement, not a personal preference. If gentle persuasion doesn't work, the Director of UG Studies and HOD can reinforce the curriculum mandate.

Real experience: From lecturing, I've learned that the best way to win people over is through demonstrated value, not through hierarchy or argument.

🔐 Scenario: Data Breach

Immediate (within 1 hour):

  • Take the data offline immediately — remove public access.
  • Assess scope — what data was exposed, for how long, and who might have accessed it.
  • Notify my line manager and ICTS security team.

Investigation:

  • Work with the student to understand how it happened — no blame, focus on fixing the process.
  • Check if similar vulnerabilities exist in other projects.

Remediation:

  • Implement access controls to prevent recurrence.
  • Create clear guidelines for researchers about data handling — file permissions, server configurations, what can and can't be public.
  • Report as required by UCT's data governance policies and POPIA (Protection of Personal Information Act).

Prevention: This scenario reinforces the need for the metadata protocols and SOPs the JD mentions. I'd prioritise creating these as part of the departmental data management framework.

🤝 Scenario: ICTS Disagreement

Key principle: ICTS are not the enemy. They have institutional responsibility for security, and their requirements exist for good reason.

Approach:

  • Understand their specific objections — Is it a particular vulnerability? A deployment concern? A policy conflict? Getting specific turns a "no" into a "not yet, because..."
  • Collaborate on a solution — Can the tool be modified to meet their requirements? Can we deploy it in a sandboxed environment? Can they recommend an approved alternative?
  • Document the business case — If the tool is genuinely needed for teaching or research, prepare a clear justification with risk mitigation measures.
  • Escalate respectfully — If we reach a genuine impasse, involve the HOD or the Advanced Computing Committee.

Real experience: I already liaise with ICTS through Theo Armstrong and Sibonelo Twala. We've navigated SESKA deployments and SEAT server issues by working collaboratively rather than adversarially.

📊 Scenario: Proving Value to the Department

I'd prepare a report structured around the 6 KPAs with measurable outcomes:

  • Teaching: Number of courses supported, student satisfaction scores, new digital exercises developed, tutors trained.
  • Research: Number of researchers supported, data pipelines built, HPC access setup, datasets managed.
  • SEAT: System uptime %, new features delivered, user satisfaction, registration cycle performance.
  • Infrastructure: Software/hardware acquisition, security improvements, cost savings from better resource management.
  • Digital Lab progress: Steps taken toward the larger CIV Digital Lab vision, partnerships established.

I'd also include qualitative feedback from course conveners, researchers, and students. Quantitative metrics tell the "what"; qualitative feedback tells the "so what."

🌍 Scenario: External Data Integration

Step-by-step approach:

  1. Requirements gathering: Meet with the research team to understand exactly what data they need, at what frequency, and how they'll use it.
  2. API investigation: Explore SANRAL's data access options — do they have a REST API? Data exports? Do we need a formal data sharing agreement?
  3. Pipeline design: Build an ETL pipeline in Python — extract from the SANRAL endpoint, transform/clean the data, load into our PostgreSQL database. I'd use scheduled tasks (cron on Linux) for regular automated refreshes.
  4. Data quality: Implement validation checks — sensor outages, missing values, outliers. Build monitoring to alert when data quality drops.
  5. Documentation: Create metadata records and data dictionaries so researchers understand the data's provenance and limitations.

Real experience: I've built web scraping and data extraction tools (CSV, HTML, BigQuery), published Python data processing packages on PyPI, and created ETL pipelines as part of my IBM Data Engineering certification. I also have experience with Google Maps APIs and REST APIs from the Ecoride project.

🧠 Scenario: The "Vision" Question

Vision: "In 5 years, I'd want the Department of Civil Engineering to be the leading digitally-enabled civil engineering department in Africa. That means:

  • A fully operational CIV Digital Lab — not just a room with computers, but an integrated facility where students, researchers, and industry partners can access computational tools, data services, IoT test beds, and collaborative computing environments.
  • A mature digital design learning line — where every graduate leaves with competency in coding, data analysis, and computational modelling, aligned with ECSA and industry expectations.
  • Self-service data infrastructure — researchers can access, store, and analyse data through well-documented, reliable pipelines without needing to reinvent the wheel for every project.
  • Integrated AI/ML capabilities — with machine learning becoming standard in engineering research, the department should have ready-to-use tools and training for AI-assisted analysis.
  • Strong industry partnerships — through the Digital Lab, connecting with organisations like SANRAL, CSIR, and international research networks.

This aligns directly with UCT's Vision 2030 and the department's stated ambition. The CTO role is the engine that makes this vision operational."

⚡ Curveball Questions

Questions that don't fit neatly into categories but could come from any panellist.

Suggested Answer: "My PhD is on track for submission in August 2026. By the time I take up this position, I'll be in the writing-up phase or have already submitted. The PhD and the CTO role are deeply complementary — my PhD work on SESKA has given me the computational mechanics, HPC, and programming expertise that directly feeds into this role's research support function.

I see the PhD as evidence of my commitment to this department and to technical excellence, not as a competing priority. The fact that I've simultaneously maintained SEAT, lectured, and progressed my PhD shows I can manage multiple commitments effectively."

I've been at UCT since 2016, my PhD research is here, and the problems I find most meaningful — strengthening research computing, building student capacity, and developing infrastructure for a world-class civil engineering department — are here.

The CTO role isn't a step down; it's a step into exactly what I want to build my career around. The combination of teaching, research, and technical leadership is genuinely exciting. And the permanence of the role means I can invest in long-term infrastructure and relationships in a way that project-based work doesn't allow."

Suggested Answer: "My weakest area in the context of this role is probably augmented and virtual reality — which the JD mentions in the teaching support section. I have strong foundations in IoT, coding, and data science, but AR/VR is not yet in my toolkit. However, I've demonstrated that I'm a fast learner — completing over 750 hours of professional certifications in just over a year. If AR/VR becomes a priority for the digital design line, I'd invest in developing that competency systematically."

Tip: Choosing AR/VR is strategic — it's mentioned once in the JD as an aspirational item, not a core requirement. It's honest, specific, and immediately followed by evidence of your ability to close skill gaps quickly.

Suggested Answer: "I'd share my perspective with evidence — not as a challenge, but as input. Technical decisions should be based on facts: performance data, security implications, cost, and user impact. I'd present the tradeoffs and my recommendation, but ultimately respect the decision-making authority of the line manager or committee.

If the decision leads to a problem I predicted, I wouldn't say 'I told you so' — I'd focus on fixing it and documenting the lesson learned for future decisions. Building trust over time means your input carries more weight in future discussions."
